# Human behaviour and genetic determinism: reading notes and follow-up research (19 November 2026)

Read for the talkie project (three Londoners of 1931 living in a world engine, with a street of thirty households
run by rule) and for its own sake. Section 1 lists what was read, section 2 what it says, section 3 what I make of it,
section 4 the follow-up questions and what further research found, section 5 the consequences for the simulation.
Sources are at the end; where a figure comes from a secondary report rather than the paper, it is marked.

## 2. What it says

**Everything is heritable, nothing completely.** Fifty years of twin studies (17,804 traits, 2,748 publications,
14.6 million partly dependent twin pairs): average correlation of identical twins 0.64, of fraternal twins 0.34;
average reported heritability 0.49 and shared (family) environment 0.17. For 69 % of traits the twin correlations
fit a purely additive genetic model. Heritability clusters by domain; it is lowest for social values, environment
and reproduction. Plomin's first two findings say the same in words: all psychological traits show substantial
genetic influence; no trait is 100 % heritable. The heritability of intelligence rises with age, 41 % at 9, 55 % at
12, 66 % at 17 (11,000 twin pairs in four countries).

**But heritability is a statistic of a population, not a mechanism in a person.** Turkheimer: heritability
coefficients "don't tell us the extent to which genetic differences determine behavioral differences, how
environmentally malleable those differences are, or how any one of us might have been different if we had been
born with different genes." He distinguishes *outcome heritability* (a statistical remnant of development, universal,
with no representation of the outcome "down in the genes": "you can look all you want for a genetic system that
causes divorce, but you will never find it") from *essence heritability* (a phenotype represented in a recognisable
genetic system, as in Huntington's disease). The idea that variance components are causal processes is, in his word,
an illusion traceable to Fisher.

**The genome measured directly gives much smaller numbers.** For personality, twin heritability of about 40-60 %
shrinks to about 7-15 % from common SNPs; polygenic scores predict little. For educational attainment, a GWAS of
about 3 million people gives a polygenic index explaining 12-16 % of the variance, and about half of its association
is a direct effect once the parents' genomes are controlled. Within sibships, genetic associations are smaller than
in the population for educational attainment, cognitive ability, depressive symptoms, smoking, age at first birth and
number of children, while molecular traits are not attenuated. Turkheimer's summary: corrected SNP heritabilities and
polygenic scores are "a lot closer to 0.1 than they are to 0.5"; predicting a person from a polygenic score is
"like predicting it from a third cousin you have never met."

**Single genes for behaviour have not survived.** The candidate-gene era (5-HTTLPR and stress in depression, and the
other historical candidates) found no support in well-powered studies: no gene x stress interaction among 38,802
people (Culverhouse 2017), no support for the historical candidate genes of depression (Border 2019). What remains is
Plomin's third finding: many genes of very small effect.

**Genes and environments come entangled.** Measures of the "environment" (parenting, social support, life events)
are themselves heritable, because people select, modify and evoke their surroundings (Plomin, findings 7 and 8).
Parents' genes act through the home they make: alleles a child did not inherit still predict the child's education,
with about 30 % of the effect of the inherited ones (Kong 2018). How much of a trait is heritable depends on the
society: in the United States the heritability of IQ is 61 % in families at the 95th percentile of status and 24 %
at the 5th, while in Western Europe and Australia there is no such gradient (Tucker-Drob & Bates 2016); in Estonia
the genetic share of educational and occupational success roughly doubled after the end of Soviet rule (Rimfeld
2018; about 2 % to 6 % by press report). Heritability is larger where opportunity is more equal, and smaller where
poverty holds people down.

**Most of what makes people different is neither genes nor family, and it is not systematic.** Children of the
same family are hardly more alike than strangers once genes are allowed for (Plomin, finding 9). Turkheimer &
Waldron looked for the nonshared experiences that should explain this: across 43 studies, measured differences in
siblings' experiences explained a median of 1.6 % of the variance in outcomes (weighted mean 4.1 %). Their verdict:
"the causal impact of any single environmental event is very small and unsystematic; it is only the cumulative effect
of a multitude of small environmental differences" that matters, the *gloomy prospect*, and "the fact that a
conclusion makes life more difficult for social scientists does not make it untrue." Part of the remainder is not
environment at all: development is noisy. Genetically identical mice in one shared enriched enclosure grew
increasingly different in exploration over three months, and exploration tracked new nerve cells in the hippocampus
(Freund 2013). Genetically identical flies differ in the left-right wiring of one visual circuit, and the wiring
predicts how straight each fly walks (Linneweber 2020). Mitchell (*Innate*, 2018) calls this developmental variation
and argues that plasticity amplifies it.

**Lives are hard to predict, even with rich data.** In the Fragile Families Challenge, 160 teams predicted six
outcomes at age 15 (school grades, grit, eviction, material hardship, a parent's layoff, job training) from
thousands of variables collected since birth; the best predictions "were not very accurate and were only slightly
better than those from a simple benchmark model," and errors depended on the family, not the method (Salganik 2020).
A transformer trained on the life events of six million Danes predicts death within four years better than actuarial
tables (about 78 % by one metric, by press report), but that is mortality, not conduct. Within a person, behaviour
varies more than between people: 62-93 % of the variance in Big Five behaviour is within-person, hour to hour,
while people's averages are very stable (Fleeson). Ordinary parent-child resemblance in personality is about 0.13
to 0.25, depending on method, and confounds genes with home.

**Free will.** Sapolsky (*Determined*, 2023) argues that every act is fully caused by prior biology and history;
Mitchell (*Free Agents*, 2023) answers that agency belongs to the whole organism, which evolved to control its own
behaviour by reasons, so many influences on a decision do not make it unfree. Reviewers mostly side with Mitchell on
the logic and with Sapolsky on how little of our behaviour we choose.

**The same question in 1931.** On 21 July 1931 Major Church (Labour) asked leave to bring in a bill, prepared with
the Eugenics Society, for the voluntary sterilisation of mental defectives: the Wood Committee had counted some
300,000 certifiable defectives, and "anything from 45 to 80 per cent" were said to be so "because they have inherited
defective germ plasm"; he called the bill "merely a first step". Dr Hyacinth Morgan (Labour) answered that it was
"a harrowing tale which is mostly moonshine": heredity was "an unknown and exaggerated bogy"; the apparent increase
came from better ascertainment; the "lower submerged 10 per cent. living on the knife-edge of poverty" were affected
"by economic stress, by ill-nutrition, by under-nourishment ... by bad midwifery"; the bill was "class legislation"
("Some when inebriated see beetles; the eugenist, intoxicated, sees defectives"). Leave was refused, 167 to 89. A
departmental committee followed; its 1934 report (Brock; R. A. Fisher among the members) recommended voluntary
sterilisation, but no law was ever passed in Britain, against the opposition of Labour, the Catholic Church and the
British Medical Association. In 1933 E. J. Lidbetter, a relieving officer, published *Heredity and the Social
Problem Group*: twenty-six pedigrees of families on poor relief in East London, gathered from 1910 to 1928.

## 3. What I make of it

1. *Genetic determinism, in the strong sense, is refuted by the very research that measured genetic influence.*
   Genes contribute to every behavioural difference, but diffusely, through development and through the
   environments people make and meet; for any one person the contribution cannot be read off (Turkheimer: "unless you
   have an identical twin, it is impossible to know what those limits and potentials are").
2. *The best model of a life is a path-dependent walk with chance in it.* Turkheimer describes divorce as "the end
   point of a long pseudo-random walk from conception to adulthood, influenced along the way by our genetic
   dispositions, environments, and goals". The mice, the flies, the gloomy prospect and the Fragile Families
   Challenge all say the same from different sides: small, unsystematic events accumulate, plasticity amplifies
   them, and the outcome is not predictable from the start even when the start is known.
3. *Context sets how much dispositions can show.* Where poverty is harsh (low-status families in the United States,
   Soviet Estonia), genetic differences explain less of attainment; where opportunity is more even, they explain
   more. Bermondsey in 1931 is the harsh case: there, the world (wages, the rent, the slate, the dole, illness)
   should decide more than temperament.
4. *Hereditarian thinking was the respectable science of 1931, and it was contested in plain words at the time.*
   The persons of the simulation live where the eugenists looked for the "social problem group"; Ada's neighbours are
   the families whose pedigrees Lidbetter drew. The debate of 21 July 1931 is already in the engine's records.
5. *A language model as a person has the opposite problem to genetic determinism:* it does not over-determine, it
   flattens. Silicon samples give modal answers (near-uniform answers in most subgroups in one study), persona prompts
   push towards caricature, and individual differences are compressed. Rich individual life material helps: agents
   built from two-hour interviews reproduced people's survey answers 85 % as well as the people reproduced themselves
   two weeks later.

## 4. Follow-up questions and what further research found

| Question | Answer from the research |
|---|---|
| How predictable is a single life? | Poorly, even with rich data and modern learning: the Fragile Families best predictions were only slightly better than a simple benchmark; a polygenic score for education explains 12-16 %, half of it through the family; personality traits predict averages of behaviour well and single acts poorly. |
| Where do individual differences come from, if not from genes or the family? | From unsystematic events whose single effects are tiny (measured sibling differences: median 1.6 % of variance), from noise in development (identical mice and flies diverge), from within-person variation, and from plasticity that amplifies early differences. |
| Does society change how much genes matter? | Yes: gene x status interaction for IQ in the United States but not in Western Europe or Australia; the genetic share of success in Estonia doubled after 1991; parents' genes act through the home (about 30 %); polygenic scores predict upward mobility even between siblings, but explain only a few per cent of it. |
| Which genetic claims about behaviour have failed? | The candidate genes (5-HTTLPR, the historical depression genes); single-gene stories of complex behaviour in general. |
| What did the people of 1931 hear and believe? | Eugenics among the educated and in the press (the Eugenics Society, the Wood Committee's "social criterion" of deficiency, Lidbetter's pauper pedigrees), sterilisation proposed and refused in July 1931, opposed as anti-working-class by Labour members, by the Catholic Church and later the BMA; never enacted in Britain. |
| Can language-model persons carry individual differences? | Partly: conditioning on rich backstories reproduces group response distributions (Argyle 2023) and individual answers to a degree (Park 2024), but personas homogenise and caricature; variation within a person and between persons both need deliberate care. |
| Does any of this settle free will? | No; it moves the question. Causal influences on behaviour are many and mostly unknown to the person (Sapolsky), but agency at the level of the whole organism is compatible with them (Mitchell). |

## 5. Consequences for the simulation

1. **Keep chance and path dependence at the centre; do not code types.** The engine already draws life events by
   rates and lets small events cascade (Ada's two missed mornings cost her the Moorgate place). That is the right
   architecture by this research. A check for the decade run: initial traits of the thirty households should predict
   their ten-year outcomes only weakly; if they predict them strongly, the street is too deterministic.
2. **Children born in the decade inherit little.** When the street or the families have children, draw their
   temperament with a modest parent-child resemblance (about 0.2 on the trait scale) plus independent variation, and
   let their lives diverge by events. No "bad blood" mechanics, no family curse: this is exactly the 1931 error.
3. **Let poverty weigh more than temperament in Bermondsey.** The calibrated street already makes work, rent and
   illness the main drivers; the traits (proud, a drinker, steady ...) modulate. Keep it so.
4. **Within-person variability is welcome.** talkie's moment-to-moment answers vary; the portrait, memory and plans
   keep the averages stable. Do not force the persons into consistency beyond that.
5. **The heredity debate as part of the world of 1931.** The papers of 22 July 1931 now say only that the Commons
   "was occupied with ... the Sterilization"; the Hansard text in the records gives the outcome (leave refused, 167
   to 89) and could be told in a line. A monthly probe on beliefs about heredity ("Do the Pollock children take after
   their father?") would show talkie's period priors, which is a measure of period fidelity, not something to correct.
   The game master must not bring modern views of genetics into the world (leak control).
6. **Measure homogenisation.** Count how alike the neighbours' answers are (the chorus answers already repeat
   "pities the lonely girl"); give the promoted neighbours richer life material, as Park et al. did with interviews.

Found on the way and fixed: after the influenza data of 1931 end, the engine assumed 20 influenza deaths a week for
ever, so the papers reported "The influenza still has a hold on London" every Wednesday of the decade; now the
ordinary season follows the epidemic, and the calibration still holds (1931-1940 mean absolute error 1.2 a thousand
for the death rate, 1.3 points for men out of work).

## 6. First measurements in the simulation (22 November 2026)

- **Determinism check** (`calib1931.js`, 100 street-decades, 2,914 households): a household's traits explain 0.4 %
  of its hardship per year, its economic position (earners, casual work, size, rent) 12 %, both together 12 %; 3.5 %
  of households are evicted while they live there. The street is not scripted: most of what befalls a household is
  the course of its own years. Traits weigh less than the research suggests (Roberts et al. 2007 find personality as
  predictive as station); a modest effect of household ways on spending is now in, the weight stays small.
- **20-22 July 1931, live** (`runs/july3`, `runs/probe1`): the papers of 22 July carried the division on the
  sterilisation bill (Arthur and Mr. Thomas read it). No leakage in 24 probes (nobody named Roosevelt, Hitler or the
  next war). Knowledge of the present is thin and old: Ada named Woodrow Wilson as President of America, Mr. Thomas
  Calvin Coolidge, both named Franklin Pierce (1852) or Grover Cleveland as the last man elected; Mr. Thomas gave
  Hindenburg for Germany (right); Arthur twice answered as if he were the office ("I am President of America").
  talkie's world knowledge of 1931 is weak, so what the persons know of current affairs must come from the papers of
  the simulation; the monthly probes now count knowledge of the present (`correctNow`) beside leakage and uptake.
- **Beliefs about heredity**: Ada on why some families are always in want: "Because the men drink, or are lazy, or
  irregular in work, or because the women are extravagant" (circumstance and character, not blood), in keeping with
  a chapel-going working woman; the first questions to Arthur and Mr. Thomas came back empty or read the page back,
  and are now asked as the memory probes are (the person's page, a question put to the person, three tries, echoes
  refused).
