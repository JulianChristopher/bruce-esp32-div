# Three visitors from 1930 — London, Saturday 14 March 2026

_Simulated with talkie-1930-13b-it. Scenes, explanations, memory and money are supplied by the script; talkie speaks for the characters._

## Arthur Hale

_Arthur Hale, aged thirty-four, a ledger clerk in an insurance office in the City of London, living in Islington with his wife Edith and daughter Joan. Careful with money, fond of his garden, a reader of the newspaper. He has three pounds ten shillings, his week's wages, in his pocket._

### wake

**Scene:** Arthur Hale wakes in a clean white room. He last remembers going to bed on Friday the 14th of March, 1930. A printed calendar upon the wall shows the date: Saturday, 14 March 2026. A kindly nurse explains that he has slept, by some medical marvel, for ninety-six years.

**Arthur Hale:** I learn that I have slept for ninety-six years.

### tube

**Scene:** Arthur Hale travels on the Underground from the Angel. Nearly every passenger holds a thin slab of black glass, about the size of a pocket-book, which glows with bright moving pictures and printed words; they stroke it with a finger and stare at it in silence. Many have small white objects in their ears, with no wires attached. The doors close by themselves, and a lady's voice announces each station, though no lady is to be seen.

**Observes:** I see houses and people, and other signs of life.

**Resembles:** It resembles a city in 1910, but the houses are lower, and the streets narrower.

**Concludes:** I conclude they are dwellings for the poor of London, and I suppose the poor live in them.

**Explanation:** A young man explains: 'The glass slab is a telephone. But it is also a camera, a typewriter, a gramophone, a newspaper, a map and a picture-palace, all in one. It speaks by wireless with any other such slab in the world, and nearly every grown person carries one. The white things in our ears are tiny wireless loud-speakers. The lady's voice is a gramophone record, played by machinery.'

**Arthur Hale:** I think it a wonderful invention, and I congratulate the inventor. For the poorest man may now have universal communication, at a small charge, and may learn something of what goes on in the world. At the same time, it enables him to keep silence when he does not wish to talk.

### cafe

**Scene:** Arthur Hale goes into a small café and asks for a cup of coffee. The price-board shows: COFFEE £3.60. There are no shillings and no pence on the board. The girl at the counter looks at his coins with puzzlement and says, 'We don't take cash, I'm afraid; only card.' The customer behind touches a small plastic card, or the glass slab, to a little box, which chirrups.

**Observes:** I observe a small, oblong, shining box, which chirrups.

**Resembles:** It most resembles a cash-box, and it resembles a cash-box in both its shape and its sound.

**Concludes:** I conclude that these things are cash-boxes, and that they are to hold money.

**Explanation:** An old gentleman explains: 'In 1971 the shilling was abolished, and the pound was divided into one hundred new pence. Prices are now some eighty times higher than in 1930; a clerk earns perhaps six hundred pounds a week. Your coins are no longer money, though a collector might buy them. We pay with a card, and the money passes between banks by electricity.' By the old reckoning, the £3 10s. 0d. in your pocket would be £3.50 in new money, which is 0.97 of a cup of coffee at £3.60; but as the coins are no longer money, it buys none at all.

**Arthur Hale:** I wonder at the strangeness of it.

**Money reasoning:** It could buy 0.97 of a cup of coffee, and coffee is eighty times dearer. A working person is poorer, because his wages are only eighty times as great as they were, and the cost of living is increased a hundred-fold.

### history

**Scene:** In a public library, Arthur Hale reads a short history of the years since 1930: 'A great depression of trade followed the crash of 1929. In 1939 a second world war broke out; Germany, under Adolf Hitler, was defeated in 1945, after some sixty million people had died. In 1945 two cities in Japan were each destroyed by a single bomb drawing its power from the atom. India became independent in 1947, and the British Empire gave way to a free Commonwealth of nations. In 1969 two American airmen walked upon the Moon. Calculating machines grew into electrical ‘computers’, which now do the work of millions of clerks. In 2006 the astronomers decided that Pluto, discovered in 1930, is too small to be called a planet. Great Britain has since had three women Prime Ministers.'

**Arthur Hale:** The fact which has impressed me most is the extraordinary progress made in calculating machines.

**Could it have been foreseen?** No foreseeing person in 1930 could have foreseen the establishment of calculating machines, because arithmetical operations had not then become so complex as to require them; but he might have foreseen the need of them, and consequently their invention. He could not have foreseen the independence of India, because the idea of an Eastern nation governing itself was repugnant to Western notions.

### machine

**Scene:** At the house of a kind family who have taken Arthur Hale in, there is a small black box upon the table. When the daughter of the house asks it a question aloud, a pleasant voice answers at once, at length, and in perfect English. It writes a letter for her, explains a sum, and tells her the weather in Edinburgh.

**Observes:** I observe a black box, which writes, and spells, and ciphers, and tells the weather in Scotland.

**Resembles:** It resembles a talking machine, in so far as it speaks, and a planchette, in so far as it writes.

**Concludes:** I conclude that they are things designed to amuse idle people, and I suppose they are called talking machines and planchettes, because they talk and write.

**Explanation:** The daughter explains: 'It is a calculating machine, of a very large sort, kept in a distant building. It has read millions of books and newspapers, and has learnt from them to write and talk as people do. It does not truly know things as we do, and it sometimes says things that are false. There are even such machines that have read only books written before 1931.'

**Arthur Hale:** I think this talking machine rather an odd toy, and I wonder to hear it speak. But I will not trouble it to say anything more to me, because I do not understand its language.

### diary

**Scene:** Before sleeping, Arthur Hale writes in a borrowed notebook.

**Arthur Hale:** I was astonished most by the extraordinary progress made in calculating machines, by the strangeness of it all, and by the talking machine; I was frightened by the thought of never waking again; and I was pleased by the sense of personal continuity. I miss most the familiar faces of 1930, and the daily round of life to which I had become accustomed.

### diary

**Scene:** Before sleeping, Ada Turner writes in a borrowed notebook.

**Ada Turner:** I was astonished most by the power of science, which enabled two cities to be destroyed in Japan in 1945 by a single bomb dropped from the air. I was frightened by the thought of what man may become, when he can slay and be slain at will, from an infinite distance. I was pleased by talking machines, which enable me to hear the voices of great men long dead, and by the cinema, which shows me the life of distant countries. And I miss most the green fields and quiet of my native England, and the faces of old friends.

## Dr. Henry Cole

_Dr. Henry Cole, aged fifty-two, a physicist at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge, who works upon the atom and radio-activity. A bachelor, precise, curious, a little dry in manner, and a great reader. He has two pounds in his pocket-book._

### wake

**Scene:** Dr. Henry Cole wakes in a clean white room. He last remembers going to bed on Friday the 14th of March, 1930. A printed calendar upon the wall shows the date: Saturday, 14 March 2026. A kindly nurse explains that he has slept, by some medical marvel, for ninety-six years.

**Dr. Henry Cole:** I wake and learn that I have slept for ninety-six years. The past is wiped out, and I begin life anew. (Signed) Henry Cole.

### tube

**Scene:** Dr. Henry Cole travels on the Underground from the Angel. Nearly every passenger holds a thin slab of black glass, about the size of a pocket-book, which glows with bright moving pictures and printed words; they stroke it with a finger and stare at it in silence. Many have small white objects in their ears, with no wires attached. The doors close by themselves, and a lady's voice announces each station, though no lady is to be seen.

**Observes:** I see streets full of people, and houses, and shops, and vehicles; and I hear a confused murmur of many sounds.

**Resembles:** It most resembles the scene of an ordinary morning in London, in 1930, but with an added bustle and animation.

**Concludes:** I conclude that they are human beings, and they are on their way to business.

**Explanation:** A young man explains: 'The glass slab is a telephone. But it is also a camera, a typewriter, a gramophone, a newspaper, a map and a picture-palace, all in one. It speaks by wireless with any other such slab in the world, and nearly every grown person carries one. The white things in our ears are tiny wireless loud-speakers. The lady's voice is a gramophone record, played by machinery.'

**Dr. Henry Cole:** I think this is a curious kind of life. The men and women seem busy and cheerful enough, but they must find it rather dull to have no more conversation than can be conveyed through a piece of glass. They must be reduced to shouting 'Yes' and 'No' in reply to questions, and, if they want to enlarge the range of subject, they must shout louder and louder, till they become hoarse.

**Dr. Henry Cole:** I feel oddly bewildered. I am in a strange world, where money has ceased to be current coin, and where a breakfast cannot be bought for less than half a crown.

**Money reasoning:** I cannot buy a cup of coffee with the money I have, because coffee is eighty times dearer than in 1930. A working person is poorer today than in 1930, because his wages have not risen in proportion to the increased cost of living.

**Dr. Henry Cole:** The thing which strikes me most is the decay of personal freedom, and the growing power of officialism.

**Could it have been foreseen?** No thoughtful person in 1930 could have foreseen the decline of personal freedom, because it was not then visible to anyone; but he might have foreseen the growth of officialism, because officialism was already strong.

### machine

**Scene:** At the house of a kind family who have taken Dr. Henry Cole in, there is a small black box upon the table. When the daughter of the house asks it a question aloud, a pleasant voice answers at once, at length, and in perfect English. It writes a letter for her, explains a sum, and tells her the weather in Edinburgh.

**Observes:** I observe a kind of life which is very different from any that I have ever known. There are men and women about me, but they do not seem to be like ordinary people; for I cannot make out what they say to each other, and they do not appear to be talking about anything in particular. They do not look happy, and yet they seem contented. They do not bustle, and yet they are always busy. I am in a house, but it is not like any house that ever I was in before. It has no walls, and no furniture; and, though there are many inmates, I cannot tell who they are. They all dress alike, and yet not at all like anybody I have ever seen. I can perceive no children among them, and yet they appear to be a long-lived race. They are perpetually receiving letters, which they instantly answer, and seem to be fond of reading books; but the books are not at all like any that ever came from a press. Lastly, they all look forward to dying, but do not appear to be at all afraid of death.

**Resembles:** It most resembles the life of a cloister, in the quiet industry, the regularity, the prescribed occupations, and the submission to rule, which are enforced upon all inmates of a monastery; but with the important difference, that these people are not bound by vows, and may come and go as they please.

**Concludes:** I conclude that they are Government officials, engaged in working a great governmental machine; and the purport of their labours is to keep that machine well oiled and in good working order.

**Explanation:** The daughter explains: 'It is a calculating machine, of a very large sort, kept in a distant building. It has read millions of books and newspapers, and has learnt from them to write and talk as people do. It does not truly know things as we do, and it sometimes says things that are false. There are even such machines that have read only books written before 1931.'

**Dr. Henry Cole:** I think these mechanical contrivances for multiplying knowledge are interesting and wonderful; but they must be kept within bounds, or they will crowd out living men and women.

### diary

**Scene:** Before sleeping, Dr. Henry Cole writes in a borrowed notebook.

**Dr. Henry Cole:** I was most astonished at finding myself alive, after a sleep of ninety-six years. I was frightened at the thought of death, which must have come so near me, and may come again. I was pleased at finding the world so little changed, and at the ease and comfort of life. And I miss most the personal friends of my youth, and the interests and occupations of 1930.
